A mere thought experiment at this time, this is an exploration of the idea of limiting the [[Harmful marketing#Advertising|damage caused by advertising]] by restricting it compared to its state at the time of writing. In my opinion, the current state of advertising presents a major [[Societal issues|societal problem]].

## Rationale

- Most of it is in direct conflict of interest with its audience: the consumer wants more [bang for the buck](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/bang_for_the_buck), the ad coerces the consumer into more buck for the bang
- It creates otherwise avoidable difficulty in perception and in doing so slows down thinking and reasoning, only by a tiny amount case by case, but when it's as pervasive as today, the effect compounds
    - If you use an ad blocker on a regular basis, for a quick taste of this try surfing the internet without an ad blocker for a few days, preferably looking for something in an unfamiliar niche; if you do not, ouch, try installing [uBlock Origin (may require a browser change, the website explains why)](https://ublockorigin.com/) and feel the difference
    - Banner blindness may be an unconscious effect, but it is not free in terms of mental effort, and it increases the risk of missing native (pertaining to the website's primary purpose for the user) UI elements solely because they look like ads, which is much less of a problem when the user doesn't have to avoid anything
        - This is exacerbated by websites integrating ads in a way that are less distinguishable from their normal content, which is straight up deception
- It warps the market dynamic in favor of large budgets, drowning genuine information sharing between consumers in biased (see above on the topic of conflict of interest) advertisement
    - Steam understands this and [does not offer advertising on its platform](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/advertising)
- In pursuit of advertising effectiveness (increasing <abbr title="Click-Through Rate, the percentage of ads clicked from ads viewed">CTR</abbr> and thus allowing increase of <abbr title="Cost Per Mille, price for a thousand ad views, a common unit of purchased amount of advertising">CPM</abbr>), advertising platforms have created systems that *aggressively* deduce various bits of information about individuals
    - Including information ordinarily very private, which ad platforms are meant to avoid and publicly claim to, but enforce this *themselves*, while being beneficiaries of it, in another conflict of interest
    - The accumulated data makes for a very lucrative target for bad actors seeking to acquire knowledge about a person without their consent or legitimate public interest
    - Ludicrous increases in effectiveness from larger datasets fuel monopolization of the advertisement industry, giving the biggest players even greater leverage over the industry than their budgets alone would allow in other industries
    - Platforms typically claim that data is anonymized as it's gathered, however that process has been shown to be *reversible* on larger amounts of data even without explicit personal identifiers, making this angle of defense meaningless
        - Think how a city inhabitant's long enough location history alone very often reveals where they *live* and where they *work*, and these two points easily enough narrow down the search to a few candidates at most, without even accounting for timeline analysis
- It changes the appearance of what could otherwise be a pleasant view into a vehicle for commercial benefit, worsening quality of life and depriving cities of their unique styles in pursuit of advertising effectiveness, the techniques of which are frequently borrowed across industries, converging to ideas that work out better than others in a process similar to monopolization
    - This is sometimes somewhat combated by proactive city authorities negotiating with businesses on maintaining the intended style of the area where the business resides, but said authorities have little leverage and gradually give in to the same screaming flashy styles — and whether a city needs a style at all is uncertain, there is no consensus

## Ideas

 > ⚠️ Fair warning, all the ideas listed here are highly undercooked and probably just bad and should be taken as mere directions to explore. I'm publishing them anyway on the off-chance this gives people ideas to improve on them.

### Restrict unsolicited advertising

A large part of insidious effect from advertising lies in its pervasiveness to such a point that its subject becomes familiar enough to a person to create bias in its favor.

To combat this effect, advertising could be entirely prohibited from demonstration in places where advertising isn't inherent for the primary functioning of a place:

- Websites that aren't explicit participants in e-commerce, social networks, digital messengers, most media outlets would **not** be allowed to advertise
    - This is the key obstacle today since a lot of these are currently sustained by income from showing ads, and other funding sources have not proven to be reliable, so a direct instant ban will cause a major collapse of digital infrastructure
    - One should probably expect a commercial entity to advertise its own goods and services in its own outlets, but would not be permitted to advertise anything else
- Marketplaces and stores (including digital ones) are commerce outlets that make no sense without the ability to showcase goods not made by themselves, which can be seen as advertising, so advertising at those does not appear to be avoidable
- Question of necessity aside, a ban on street signs indicating presence and location of businesses would likely be unenforceable without massively encroaching on the concept of private property, which probably isn't worth the benefit, the visibility of those signs is very limited anyway; plus, they make for great landmarks that aid navigation within cities

### Lower ad specificity

To combat a fundamental conflict of interest where every provider of goods or services is interested in mentioning itself and not mentioning competitors, thereby creating **a lie by omission**, ads could be required to either cover a sufficiently broad category of products that a company would still have to compete on quality for advertising to be effective.

This way, new categories of products can still be advertised to further innovation in society, but even initial advertisement, when the advertiser does not yet have any competitors, serve competition and help *emerging* competitors. This *should* provoke a question «Why would advertisers do that to themselves?» and the answer to that is meant to be «They wouldn't be given a choice».

An obvious possible vulnerability here would be a company introducing a made up category of products defined in such a way to only include products by a narrow circle of providers — see [Apple's «What's a computer?» ad for iPad](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zfR_Jj4grZE) for an example what this could look like. Apple is probably the most well-known practitioner of this trick even today, previously using «the technician turned a Mac into PC by replacing parts» line of defense in court. Apple does this to  further isolate its audience from competitors' audiences in public perception in order to stifle competition.

The key to this measure working as intended would be the regulatory authority responsible for tracking compliance and defining specific criteria. This probably cannot scale to the point of requiring approval for every single ad, but existing framework of enforcement for misleading advertising, handling violations after they happen, might work too.

## Problems

### Financial dependency

As outlined above, advertising industry has gotten so pervasive that tons of projects that modern society has gotten used to today almost entirely *depend* on income from advertising, we have entire professions based on this, such as "content creators", though some of them realize how dangerous this situation is and diversify their businesses into other markets.

A direct ban on advertisement would collapse many businesses and will force many individuals to take a major career turn, to the point of starting a new one from scratch, likely disadvantaged by their age being above their competitors' on the market they may enter.

### Enforcement nightmare

Getting enforcement procedures for this right is going to be monumentally difficult without making it overly arbitrary/draconian and while still achieving the intended effect.

### Definition problems

Definitions for advertising can vary depending on who you ask, and some of the simpler definitions might include some unintended targets, which may indicate both problems with the definition or the need to adjust the approach to regulation.

What I expect to be the most common misconception: **not all advertising is bad**. It's easy to think so when all you're considering is the sense of annoyance you got out of wherever advertising has been intrusive, irrelevant, misleading or wrong. Minds have a tendency to allocate more effort to things in need of change, because that's where a mind's power needs to be applied, biasing the circle of targets toward those that caused negative experiences, while the ones that brought in positives might become unwanted collateral damage if not considered.

If we consider the very simplest definition of advertising, bordering on propaganda and manipulation, the act of delivering information to a person without their direction or consent, would include **public education**. Because sure enough, a person doesn't usually *ask* for it, but it is still generally considered to be beneficial to society. Is this definition insufficient then? Or perhaps should the same norms that we apply to material taught at schools apply to things we teach *everybody* across *all* social spaces, not just educational institutions?

There's a lot to unpack and I don't feel ready for a thorough dive into the subject, I merely note the extreme *depth* of this topic.
